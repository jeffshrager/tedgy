# Lincoln Text Adventure — Project Plan

**Ted's note to the class:** This is a real project with a real product at the end: a playable text adventure game where the player makes choices in Abraham Lincoln's world. You'll do research, make design decisions, and write actual code — all working together as a team. I'm not going to tell you exactly what to do each day. Part of the job is figuring that out as a team. I'll give you direction and check in, but the decisions are yours.

---

## What you're building

A text adventure game (think old-school "you are in a room, what do you do?") where the player experiences a key moment in Lincoln's life — maybe a debate, maybe the war, maybe something more personal. The player makes choices; the story branches. History should be accurate. The experience should be engaging.

---

## The three-day arc (demo scale)

> In a real project this would be 10–15 school days. We're compressing it to three to see how the system works. Each "day" here stands in for a phase.

---

## Day 1 — Getting organized

**The goal for today:** Leave the room knowing who does what, what the project is actually about, and what you're going to do tomorrow.

**Ted's guidance:**

Start by talking as a team — no laptops open, no writing yet. Just talk. Ask yourselves: What do you already know about Lincoln? What's interesting? What's a moment in his life that would actually make a good game? There's no right answer yet, just explore.

Then think about what it takes to make a game like this. What kinds of work are involved? (Hint: research is one. Writing is one. Programming is one. But you might find more.) Decide as a team what the roles will be — I'm not giving you a list. Figure out what roles make sense for *your* project.

Once you've agreed on roles, each person picks one (or you assign — that's up to you too). Write down: who has what role, and what that role is responsible for. That's your team contract for the project.

Finally: agree on a first rough idea for the story. It doesn't have to be final. Just a starting direction.

**What I want to see at the end of the day:**
- A written list of roles and who holds each one
- One sentence describing what your game is going to be about
- A rough plan for what each person will do over the next two days

---

## Day 2 — Research and design

**The goal for today:** Know enough about Lincoln and his world to make good story choices, and have a design that's ready to build.

**Ted's guidance:**

Split your effort: some of you should go deep on Lincoln — the facts, the timeline, the people around him, the language and feel of the era. The game needs to feel like Lincoln's world, not a modern cartoon of it. Find real details that will make the game surprising and specific.

The rest of you should be designing the game. What's the opening scene? What choices does the player face? What are the branches? Sketch this out — not as code, as a map or a flowchart or a list of scenes. Each scene needs: where we are, what the player is told, and what choices they have.

By end of day, the research team should be briefing the design team — make sure the design is historically grounded. Adjust the design based on what you learned.

**What I want to see at the end of the day:**
- A set of research notes: at least 5 facts that will actually show up in the game
- A scene map (on paper is fine) showing the full game structure: opening, branches, endings
- At least 2 endings (the game shouldn't have just one outcome)
- Every scene should have a historically-grounded detail in it

---

## Day 3 — Build and play-test

**The goal for today:** Have a playable game.

**Ted's guidance:**

Start coding. Your scene map from yesterday is your spec — build what's on it. Don't add new scenes or change the design mid-build unless something is clearly broken; finish what you planned first.

While the programmers build, the writers should be drafting the actual text for each scene — the words the player will read. Keep it tight: two or three sentences per scene, not a paragraph. Make it vivid. Use what you learned from research.

About halfway through the day, stop and play-test. Have someone who wasn't writing the code play through it cold. Watch where they get confused, where they laugh, where the story doesn't make sense. Fix the things that are actually broken. Don't try to add features — ship what you designed.

End of day: everyone plays the finished game. Team does a short debrief: what worked, what you'd change, what surprised you.

**What I want to see at the end of the day:**
- A working, playable game (even if rough)
- At least 2 distinct endings reachable by player choices
- At least one moment where historical research is clearly visible in the text
- A debrief note: one thing that went well, one thing that was hard

---

## Roles — for the team to decide

Ted isn't assigning these. But here are some functions that probably need covering — figure out who does what:

- **Research**: learns Lincoln's world deeply enough to keep the rest of the team honest
- **Writing**: drafts the actual text the player reads
- **Design**: maps out the story structure, scenes, and choices
- **Programming**: writes the code
- **Project lead**: keeps track of where the team is and what's next

Some of these might be one person. Some might be shared. One person might do two. You decide — but write it down.

---

## Ground rules

- The history has to be real. You can dramatize; you can't invent.
- The code has to run. A broken game is not a finished game.
- Everyone has to be able to explain what the game is about and how it works — not just the programmers.
- If you're stuck, talk to your team first. Then come to me.
